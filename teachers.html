Teachers VEX intro

Come drive a VEX robot: an introduction to VEX Robotics for teachersCome drive a robot.

Curious about what our students are doing in robotics in ELAC Robotics Center? Come drive a VEX robot yourself! In this low-stakes, 20-minute session, you'll get hands-on time with the controllers, try a quick mini-challenge, and see firsthand how robotics builds problem-solving, teamwork, and critical thinking across subjects. Zero technical experience required!

01 Your 20 minutes

Drive it. Try it. See it.

No slides, no code, no pressure. You will pick up a controller and move a real robot, then think about where the skills you used show up in your own classroom.

Drive

Get hands-on time with the controllers. Drive, turn and steer a VEX robot around the room.

Try

Take a quick mini-challenge with a partner. It is low-stakes, and it is fine to bump into things.

See

Notice how robotics builds problem-solving, teamwork and critical thinking, and talk about how that could connect to your subject.

Zero technical experience required.

If you can use a game controller, or even if you have never touched one, you will be fine. Students are the experts, and they will be happy to help.

02 What is VEX?

A robot kit, a game and a team.

VEX Robotics makes robot kits and runs student robotics competitions. Students use the kits to design, build and program a robot. Each season there is a new game, and teams meet at tournaments to play it.

Design and build

Teams plan a robot to solve a game challenge, then build it from kits of beams, motors, sensors and wheels.

Program

Students write code that makes the robot move and use its sensors. VEX's coding tool starts with blocks and grows into text languages such as Python. Suggested

Play and compete

Two robots work together or against each other in short Matches, and teams also take a turn alone against the clock in a Skills challenge. Official

03 Why compete?

Because a real goal changes how students work.

Building a robot is good. Building one for a Match next Saturday, with a partner team and a clock, is a different kind of learning.

A reason to get it right

A date, an audience and a score make students care about details: measuring, testing, fixing and trying again. VEX says its competitions use the motivating power of competition and robotics to help students see themselves as STEM learners. Official

Skills beyond STEM

VEX describes communication, collaboration and time-management as valuable soft skills that students practice in a fun, authentic way. Official

Students do the work

The rules say students design, build, program and plan, and adults teach and ask questions but do not do it for them. Official G2

Safe to fail

Robots break and plans fail. Students learn to test, find the problem, fix one thing and test again, and then to explain what they learned. Suggested

Teams and community

Students meet teams from other schools, work with an alliance partner and thank the volunteers who run the day. Suggested

Everyone has a role

Designer, builder, coder, notebook writer, strategist, driver and loader. Not every student needs to love coding. Suggested See the team jobs

04 Your subject

Where do these skills show up in class?

Robotics is not a separate subject. It is a place where many subjects meet. Here is what each one looks like in a robotics team, with a small idea you could try. Suggested

Problem-solvingTeamworkCritical thinkingCommunicationPerseveranceCreativityTime-management
How robotics skills connect to school subjects
SubjectWhat it looks like on a robotics teamA small idea for your class
Units of Inquiry (UOI)Robotics is a living inquiry. Students ask questions, research, test, reflect and take action. It can sit inside themes such as How the world works (forces and motion), How we organize ourselves (team roles and planning) and How we express ourselves (design and presenting). It also builds approaches to learning such as thinking, social, communication and self-management skills.Use a student's own question, such as “Why did our robot tip over?”, as a line of inquiry. Plan, test, reflect, and then take action, for example by helping at a competition.
MathMeasuring, scaling, ratios and gear ratios, angles, graphing test results, and choosing a strategy by comparing points.Use the Level Up scores (1, 3, 6, 12, 16): how many floor bags equal one top-level bag? Try the scoring calculator.
Science and physicsForce, friction, torque, balance and tipping, energy and batteries, and what sensors detect.Test two wheel types for grip, and graph the result. Ask why a tall robot tips.
Computer scienceSequences, loops, conditions, variables and debugging, from blocks to Python.Write steps that drive a robot, or a person, around a square. Then debug them.
Design and engineeringDefining a problem, prototyping, testing, and improving within limits such as size.Give a design challenge with real constraints. The Level Up robot must start inside an 11″ × 20″ × 15″ box. R3
English and writingThe engineering notebook, explaining a design, answering a judge's questions and presenting.Have students write a short “what we tried, what happened, what we learned” entry. See the notebook guide.
Languages and global learningWorking with teams from other schools and countries, and welcoming guests.Make bilingual signs or a short welcome script for a visiting team.
Social studies and communityPlanning an event, roles and responsibilities, and thanking volunteers.Treat hosting an event as a civic project. Use the host guide.
Art and designRobot looks, team branding, posters, photos and CAD drawings.Design a team logo or a pit display, and explain the choices.
PE, health and SELTeamwork, sportsmanship, staying calm under pressure and bouncing back after a bad Match.Run a short reflection: what helped our team, and what did we say when things went wrong?
Business and entrepreneurshipBudgets, timelines, roles, sponsors and communication.Plan the cost and calendar of a team season, with a countdown like the host to-do list.

05 Run a session

A 20-minute plan you can use today.

For the person leading the session. It works with one or two robots and a clear patch of floor. Suggested

Run of show for a 20-minute teacher session
TimeWhat happens
0:00 to 0:03Welcome. What VEX is, and what our students do with it.
0:03 to 0:07Controller tour. Drive, turn, and use the robot's arm or tool. Work in pairs, one driver and one coach.
0:07 to 0:16Mini-challenge. Pick one card below, and swap drivers every couple of minutes.
0:16 to 0:20Reflect. Which skill did you use? Where would it show up in your subject? What would your students need to try this?

Card 1 · Slalom

Warm-up. Drive around four cones and back, as smoothly as you can.

Skills: control, spatial thinking, patience.

Card 2 · Bean Bag delivery

Core. Pick up a Bean Bag and deliver it to a Goal or a box. A bag on the floor Goal is worth 1 point, and higher is worth more. Carry one bag at a time, just like the students. SG6

Skills: planning, trial and error, estimating.

Card 3 · Driver and Loader

Teamwork. One person drives and another places bags in the Load Zone. Swap around the 30-second mark, like the real hand-off. GG11 Use the Match timer for 60 seconds.

Skills: communication, shared goals, timing.

What you need

  • One or two robots with controllers, with charged batteries and a spare.
  • A clear floor area. The Level Up Field is 6′ × 8′, and any open space works.
  • Cones or tape, a few Bean Bags and a box or Goal.
  • A timer, sticky notes and a pen.

Keep it safe

  • One driver at a time. Everyone else stays back.
  • Hands off the robot while it is moving.
  • The facilitator handles batteries and charging.
  • Clear bags and cords from walking paths afterward.

Questions to teachers

  • Which skill did you use just now?
  • Where would it show up in your subject?
  • What would your students need before trying this?
  • What worried you, and what surprised you?

06 Questions

Questions teachers ask.

Do I need to know how to code?

No. The session is about driving and thinking, not programming. If a class later wants to code, students can start with blocks and move on to text languages. Suggested

Is robotics only for students who like technology?

No. Teams need designers, builders, coders, notebook writers, strategists, drivers and loaders. Many students find their place through a role that is not coding. See the jobs.

How does this connect to my subject?

See the table above, which lists eleven subjects with a small idea for each. Start with one idea, and ask the students what they notice.

Why do students compete instead of just building?

Competition gives students a real goal, a deadline and a partner team. It also gives them a reason to test, fix and explain their work. VEX describes competition as a way to build STEM identity and soft skills. Official

Do students do the work themselves?

Yes. The competition rules say students design, build, program and plan. Adults teach, ask questions and support. Official G2

How can I help or learn more?

Volunteer at an upcoming competition, no robot knowledge needed. See the events and sign-up links and the volunteer jobs. You can also read the coach guide to see how a team works through a season.

Where can I read the official information?

On VEX's competition page and in each season's Game Manual. vexrobotics.com/competition ↗ · Level Up Game Manual ↗
