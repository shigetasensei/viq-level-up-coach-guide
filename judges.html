Judge zone VEX IQ

Judge zone: a guide for VEX IQ judges and Judge AdvisorsListen well. Judge fairly.

You do not need robotics or engineering experience to be a judge. Judges bring attention, fairness and a real interest in student learning. This page explains the job at a VEX IQ competition: what you do, how interviews and notebooks work, the awards, and how to keep every team and student safe.

01 Your role

Judges recognize the whole season.

Robot performance is only one part of a competition. Judged awards exist so the full range of student achievement is recognized, not just match results. Official

Judge

Reviews Engineering Notebooks, holds Team Interviews, and decides the judged and nominated awards together with the other judges. Official

  • At a VEX IQ event you must be 18 or older.
  • Volunteers aged 16 to 17 may judge VEX IQ only when paired with a judge who is at least 18.
  • Judge Certification is recommended.

Judge Advisor

Manages the whole judging process and makes sure it is fair and consistent. Guides the judges, facilitates deliberations and gives the final winners to the Event Partner. Official

  • Has completed the current season's Judge Certification.
  • At least 21 years old and not enrolled in primary or secondary school.
  • Few or no conflicts of interest, and not on a team at the event.

Event Partner

Recruits the Judge Advisor and the judges, and provides a private Judges' Room. Official

  • Plan about two judges for every 8 to 10 teams for in-person judging.
  • Cannot be a judge or Judge Advisor at their own event.
  • Cannot recommend or assign judged awards, and takes no part in deliberations.

Two ideas to hold on to

Official What judges look and listen for comes down to this: students do the work, and students can explain the work. A team that did its own work can say what it tried, what it changed and why.

Two main jobs

Official Judging has two main activities, both guided by published rubrics: evaluating Engineering Notebooks and conducting Team Interviews. The scores help judges start their discussion. They do not pick winners alone.

How to become certified

Official Create a VEX PD+ account, take the Judge Certification Course, and share your certificate with Event Partners near you.

02 Before and during the day

Prepare once. Judge every team the same way.

Before the event

  • Read the game and the Game Manual.
  • Tell the Judge Advisor about any conflicts of interest.
  • Get to know the award criteria, rubrics and judging documents.
  • Complete Judge Certification (recommended).

Official

On event day

  • Evaluate notebooks and interviews.
  • Observe teams and gather evidence.
  • Take part in deliberations.
  • Give all notes and rubrics to the Judge Advisor.
  • Keep everything confidential. Encourage teams, but give no specific feedback.

Official

Key reminders

  • Every team is interviewed.
  • All judging is finished before the Finals, so decisions use only what is known by then.
  • No team receives more than one judged award.
  • Everyone keeps judging confidential, before, during and after.

Official

Recommended timeline for judging on event day
WhenWhat happens
MorningThe Judge Advisor leads orientation and training. Judges begin reading notebooks, and interviews start as early as possible so every team can be seen.
MiddayJudges keep interviewing in their groups and review their notebook scores. They begin to name preliminary award candidates.
AfternoonRemaining interviews are finished so every team has been evaluated. The Judge Advisor collects rankings and field notes, and the judges hold final deliberations.
Before FinalsThe Judge Advisor submits the final winners to the Event Partner. Notebooks go back to teams where applicable, and confidential judging materials are secured, then destroyed afterward.

03 Team interviews

A conversation, not a quiz.

In the interview, judges hear the story of the team's season from the students who lived it: the work, the problems and what they learned. It is not a presentation, and a memorized speech will not score well. Official

How it works

  • Two judges meet a team in the pits or another public place.
  • Same scheduling method and about the same length for every team.
  • Read the team's Season Summary first, then ask how and why.
  • Teams that decline an interview are not eligible for judged awards, but may still get nominated awards.

The interview rubric

Version 2.0 has two sections and six criteria, scored on what you hear, for a total of 12 points.

  • Explain Your Learning (6 points)
  • How You Worked With Others (6 points)
  • Heard, with Specifics = 2. Heard = 1. Not Yet Heard = 0.

Respect and courtesy are not scored. They are expected of every team.

What the scores mean

  • Heard (1): the team did it, in genuine general terms. All 1s totals 6 of 12.
  • With Specifics (2): a named part, a number, a source or a moment from the season.
  • Not Yet Heard (0): it was missing from the conversation. It says nothing about how hard the team worked.

Questions to ask

  • “What did you try before that?”
  • “What went wrong along the way?”
  • “How did you know it worked?”
  • “Can you show me that part in your notebook?”
  • “If you had one more week, what would you change?”

Official Ask one of these before you mark a row Not Yet Heard.

Talk with the students

  • Get on their level. Sit if they sit, and crouch with younger students.
  • Look at the student who is speaking, not your clipboard.
  • Leave silence. Count to five before rescuing a question.
  • Open easy: “Tell me about your robot.”
  • Invite quiet students by asking the group.
  • Score the content of an answer, never the delivery.

Good to know

  • Genuine sounds like teammates adding to each other's answers. Do not score down on instinct if it sounds rehearsed. Ask one question beneath the surface.
  • Students may use their notebook, like engineers using their documents.
  • If an adult answers for a student, say “I would love to hear that from the team,” and tell the Judge Advisor if it keeps happening.
  • You are not an investigator. Fill in the rubric privately after the interview.

04 Engineering Notebooks

Read for the process, not for the words.

You are reading for evidence that a team defined a problem, tried things, learned from what happened and improved. They do not need to use the exact words of the Engineering Design Process. Official

What you receive

Official A notebook plus three one-page summaries. The summaries are navigation tools and are not scored.

  • Season Summary: read this first. It points to the notebook entries behind each highlight.
  • Code Summary: how the code works, in plain language. Teams with no code skip it.
  • Credit Summary: one list of outside sources used on the robot and in the code.

The notebook rubric

Official Five sections and sixteen criteria, each scored Beginning, Developing, Proficient or Exemplary. A submitted notebook scores at least 1 on every row, so totals run from 16 to 64.

  • Notebook Organization: 16 points
  • Define: 12 points
  • Develop Solutions: 16 points
  • Optimize: 12 points
  • Reflection and Iteration: 8 points

What the scores mean

  • Proficient means the team did it. All Proficient totals 48 of 64, a solid record of a real season.
  • Exemplary goes one step past the question, such as citing a measurement or saying what they chose against.
  • Developing and Beginning describe what is missing, not how hard the team worked.

Working through a notebook

  • Start with the Season Summary and follow its pointers.
  • Score every row.
  • Use the same time limit for every notebook, and decide it before you start.
  • Have two judges review each notebook where staffing allows, and talk through gaps of more than one level.

Official

Teams differ in scale

Official A first-year VEX IQ team with two elementary students goes through the same process as an experienced team, at a different level. Score what the team in front of you did against the criteria, not against the best notebook you saw today.

Things not to do

  • Do not use AI tools, including AI detectors, to screen notebooks.
  • Do not look for problems. You are not an investigator.
  • Do not share scores. They stay within the judging team.

Official

05 Awards

Nine judged awards, two nominated.

Judged awards come from interviews, notebooks and event observations. If an event includes judging, it must offer the Excellence, Design and Judges Awards. A team may earn only one judged award at an event. Official

Nominated awards

Official Volunteers such as the Head Referee, Scorekeeping Referees and emcees, and judges, may nominate teams for positive conduct and spirit.

  • Sportsmanship: courtesy, fairness, honesty and positive conduct.
  • Energy: enthusiasm, encouragement and school spirit.

Performance awards

Official These come from match results, not judges. At VEX IQ they are the Teamwork Champion (the Finals alliance with the highest score) and the Robot Skills Champion. Winning one does not affect a team's judged award eligibility.

Use the final rankings

Official When an award has performance criteria, use the final Qualification and Skills rankings. Elimination and Finals results are not considered for judged awards. An award is not presented when no candidate meets its published criteria.

06 Deliberations

Decide together, with evidence.

Deliberation is a conversation, not a debate to be won. Judges combine rubric scores with their notes and what they saw and heard. No single number decides an award. Official

Bring

  • Your rubrics, notes and a shortlist of top teams for each award.
  • The reasons you chose them, in specifics.

Talk about teams

  • Tie every point to a published criterion.
  • Speak in specifics. “They redesigned their intake three times and every version is in the notebook” beats “they seemed strong.”
  • Represent the teams you met, unless you have a personal connection to the team.
  • Expect to be persuaded. Changing your mind is deliberation working.
  • Make room for every voice. Volume is not evidence.
  • “We didn't hear it” counts.

Choose winners

  • Rank the finalists and name alternates.
  • Compare teams against the criteria, never against each other's resources or polish.
  • Raise conduct or student-centered concerns with the Judge Advisor. They are handled outside the discussion.
  • No team gets more than one judged award. The Judge Advisor reconciles overlaps.

Everything stays in the room.

Official At the end of the event, give all notes, rubrics and ranking sheets to the Judge Advisor and delete any photos taken for judging. Winners are announced at the ceremony, never before. The discussion behind the decisions is never shared. Participating in Judging Deliberations ↗

07 Protect students

Fair to every team. Safe for every student.

Be fair

  • Judge only what you observe at this event.
  • Use the same standards, time limits and criteria for every team.
  • Do not interview or evaluate a team you have a conflict of interest with. Tell the Judge Advisor.
  • Wait until all qualification matches are complete before finalizing winners.
  • Do not try to influence an award for or against any team.

Official

Protect students

  • Work in pairs. At least two judges are present with students, and no judge is ever alone with one student.
  • Hold interviews in open, public spaces.
  • Photograph robots only, never students, and delete photos at the end.
  • Do not contact students beyond the interview. Messages go through the Judge Advisor or Event Partner.
  • An interpreter, extra time or a written answer is how a student takes part. It is never a reason to score lower.

Official

If something is wrong

  • You are not an investigator. Report what you saw to the Judge Advisor.
  • If a student is at risk, act immediately. Stop what is happening, then report promptly.
  • Never report through the person the concern is about. If it involves the Judge Advisor, go to the Event Partner.
  • If a student tells you a concern, treat it as a report, and pass it on without judging whether it is credible.

Official From the Youth Protection Policy, as summarized in "Judging Roles and Responsibilities". Conduct outside judging is handled through the Code of Conduct, not your scores.
